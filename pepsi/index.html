01 //

What we took away

How you fund it matters more than how you run it

Big companies are good at starting things and bad at stopping them. That, more than the design of the model, is what decides how well the new one works.

Keeping everyone pointed the same way is the hard part

When dozens of people touch one build, most of the cost is keeping them all working toward the same goal. Better tools do not fix this. They make it easier to add more people.

Building a company is not the same as building a prototype

A prototype shows you what is possible. A company has to survive on its own, which comes down to how it is set up more than how good the idea is. Few people have done both.

How we work//LVLON

The hard part is knowing what to build

How the work runs, week by week, and the terms. Fixed fee, an end date agreed on day one, and a written postmortem whether it worked or not.

01 //

The fair objection

"We could do a lot of this internally."

You can, and increasingly everyone can. Building has never been cheaper. That is the problem, not the answer.

When a working prototype takes days instead of quarters, the constraint moves. It stops being whether you can build the thing and becomes whether the thing is worth building. Teams lose the line back to the people they are building for and ship work that is fast, elegant, and pointed at nothing.

Human-centred design is the discipline that keeps that line intact. It is not a workshop. It is knowing which user to talk to, what to ask, how to read the answer, and when the evidence says stop. Paul founded that practice at BCG and built the Leap Network at Target to put it inside a company that did not have it, training coaches rather than staying, so it outlived the team that introduced it.

So the question is not whether you have hands. Three better ones:

  1. Of what got built last quarter, how much was tested with the people it was for before it was built?
  2. Who on the team has spoken to an end user in the last month?
  3. When something gets killed, is it because the evidence said so, or because the sponsor moved on?
02 //

What the weeks look like

Know what to fund, and when to stop

6 to 8 weeks // Two milestones

Weeks 1–2
We learn how ideas actually get funded and stopped today, by sitting in the real meetings rather than reading the written process.
Weeks 3–5
We write the stages, what each one has to prove, and the rules that end a project. Tested on real projects you have running now, not in theory.
Weeks 6–8
We run it once with you on real decisions. You keep the model and the words to defend it upstairs.

Proof that people want it

60 days // Milestone-gated

Weeks 1–2
We find and talk to the people the thing is for. The problem gets written from what they say, not from the brief.
Weeks 3–6
Build, test, rebuild. Something working in front of real users every week. Never production grade.
Weeks 7–8
A clear recommendation, the proof behind it, and a handover so the next one does not start from scratch.

A new business that runs on its own

Staged // First stage 90 days

Weeks 1–4
The idea and the market it needs, backed by customers who say they would pay.
Weeks 5–9
Build enough to sell. First conversations with real buyers, not a pitch deck.
Weeks 10–13
How it would be set up. What it takes to stand on its own, and whether it should.
